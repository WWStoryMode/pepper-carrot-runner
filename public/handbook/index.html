Part one — the parts every 2D game has

01

The loop

A game is a program that never finishes. It does three things, over and over, about sixty times a second.

Each pass is a frame. At 60 frames per second you have about 16 milliseconds for all three. Go over budget and the game stutters.

The consequence designers should hold onto: nothing in a game is continuous. A jump is not a smooth arc, it is 51 discrete positions. Two things touching is a test performed at instants — which is why a fast object can pass through a wall between two frames without ever being inside it.

02

Why the clock is separate from the picture

A real bug from the original 2016 game, and the most important technical idea here.

It moved Pepper vertically once per drawn frame, with no reference to how much time had passed. On a 60 Hz monitor that gives the intended jump. On a 144 Hz monitor the code runs 144 times a second, gravity applies more than twice as often, and the jump collapses to a hop. The game played differently on different monitors. Not looked — played.

Why a designer should care

Your tuning values are only meaningful if the simulation clock is fixed. Otherwise "jump height" is not a property of your game — it is a property of the player's hardware.

There is a second reason. If the game is paused for two seconds — a background tab, a laptop lid — should it then race through two seconds of missed hazards? Almost never. This game drops the backlog and resumes in real time, so you do not come back to find you died during lunch.

03

The rules, and the picture

The most useful structural decision in this project: the rules of the game are kept separate from what it looks like.

Why it matters to a designer:

  • The rules can be tested without running the game. This project verifies its jump height by arithmetic in under a second — no browser, no waiting, no looking.
  • You can change the look without touching the game. Reskinning risks nothing.
  • Bugs get an address. "Pepper died unfairly" is a rules problem. "Pepper died and the animation was wrong" is a picture problem. You know which conversation to have.

04

Sprites, atlases and animation

A sprite is an image drawn at a position. An animation is a list of images shown in sequence, each with a duration. This project transcribes the original's timings exactly — including the detail that Carrot's run is faster than Pepper's, because a small cat takes quicker steps.

Why art gets packed

Sending an image to the graphics card is cheap. Switching between images is expensive. So at build time all 140 sprite files are packed into a couple of large sheets — a texture atlas — with a data file recording where each frame landed.

The original made an instructive mistake here. Its atlas was packed without regard to grouping, so a single run cycle was split across three different sheets — meaning the most frequently drawn animation in the game forced a switch on nearly every frame. Pack related frames together.

Practical

Hand your programmer loose, well-named source files — pepper_run_01.png — and let the build combine them. Never hand over a hand-assembled sheet; you will want to change one frame later.

05

Levels made of tiles

Rather than one enormous image, levels are built from a grid of small squares — tiles, 95×95 pixels here. A level is then a spreadsheet of numbers saying which tile goes where. Compact, editable, and it gives you collision almost free: the game already knows which cells are solid.

Levels were authored in Tiled, the standard free tile editor. The original did something clever worth copying: enemies, potions and hazards are not separate objects — they are tiles on hidden layers, where each tile type carries properties like type: enemy or obstacle: deadly.

Practical

This means your level editor is the only tool you need. Painting a spider is the same gesture as painting a brick.

06

Collision

Nearly every 2D game answers "are these touching?" with boxes — not the character's outline, a rectangle approximating it. Pepper's sprite is 380 pixels tall; the box that actually collides is 115×208 and sits inside her. That is standard, and generous: a hitbox slightly smaller than the character makes a game feel fair, because near-misses read as misses.

Two ideas do most of the work in a runner.

One-way platforms

You jump up through a platform and land on top of it, but never fall through from above. Nearly every platformer does this, because the alternative — bonking your head on every ledge — feels terrible.

Sweeping

Testing only "where is she now" misses fast movement: at full falling speed Pepper covers 26 pixels between frames and can pass clean through a platform. So the test covers the whole path travelled since the last frame, not just the endpoint. The original never solved this and instead capped falling speed so it could not happen — a workaround its author labelled with a FIXME.

07

The camera

The camera decides what the player can see, which makes it a design tool rather than plumbing. It rarely follows the character exactly — that produces nauseating jitter. It lags, smooths, and often deliberately looks ahead.

A real note from this project's playtesting:

"The camera is currently too close to the character and we cannot see the floor or gap when we are dropping from height."

Landing was guesswork, because the player could not see where they would land. The fix was to show more world and bias the view downward while falling. A camera that hides the information a decision needs makes the game unfair, however good the physics underneath.

08

What a character is doing

A character is in exactly one state at a time, with defined transitions.

It also exposes design decisions. In the original you could only double-jump while still rising — step off a ledge, or pass the top of your jump, and the second jump was gone. Players read that as a bug rather than a rule. Here an air jump is available whenever you have one left. One arrow on the diagram; a large difference in feel.

Part two — what a runner does differently

09

The contract

The player does not control moving forward. They only control jumping. That is the genre, and everything follows from it.

Because forward motion is automatic, you cannot build a challenge around going somewhere — the player is always going there. The only decision is when to press, and the only skill is timing. So all design pressure moves into what is in front of the player, and when they can see it.

A platformer designer asks "how do I make this jump interesting?" A runner designer asks how to make the timing interesting, because the jump is always the same jump.

The upside

This is why a runner is a superb first game. There is exactly one verb. Make it feel excellent, and the entire rest of the project is content and systems rather than more mechanics.

10

The jump envelope

The most important idea here. Measure your jump, then design everything against that measurement.

Pepper's jump — measured by running the simulation
PropertySingleDouble
Rises to325 px650 px
Airtime0.85 s1.5 s
Horizontal reach340 px600 px
In tiles (95 px)3.66.3
Second jump taken at the apex. Run speed 400 px/s.

Those numbers are the physical limits of your level. Every gap, every obstacle, every platform height must sit inside them — not approximately, but with margin.

What the envelope then permits
Widest gap the game will generate3 tiles · 285 px
Single-jump reach340 px
Deliberate safety margin55 px
Tallest obstacle face1 tile · 95 px

That 55-pixel margin is the whole point. A gap that is exactly clearable is only clearable by a player who jumps at exactly the right pixel, and it feels like a trap even when cleared. Obstacle faces at 95 px against a 325 px rise sit so far inside the envelope that hitting one is unambiguously a mistake the player made, rather than a demand they could not meet.

Do this first

Write your envelope on a card and pin it up. "Max gap 3 tiles, max step 1 tile." Every layout decision gets checked against it. This is why the jump must be tuned before any level is authored — every level you have already made assumes the jump you had when you made it.

11

The reaction budget

The player can only respond to what they have seen. So how long do they get?

Warning time
Visible world ahead of the player≈1,300 px
Run speed400 px/s
Warning available≈3.3 s
Under Time Distortion, at 2.8×<1.2 s

Three seconds sounds generous, but it covers everything: seeing the obstacle, recognising it, deciding, and pressing. Human reaction to an expected visual cue is around 250 ms; to something that must be identified first, considerably more.

So obstacles must be readable at a glance — not detailed, distinguishable. Deadly green sludge must not resemble decorative green scenery. And note what speeding the game up does to that budget: it is precisely why acceleration here is a player-triggered spell. The player chose to make it harder, and knows it.

12

Forgiveness is a feature

The biggest difference between the original and this version is invisible in a screenshot. The original had no input forgiveness at all. Miss the ledge by one frame — a sixtieth of a second — and you were falling with no recourse.

Two standard techniques
Coyote time — jump still works after leaving a ledge0.10 s
Jump buffering — early press remembered until landing0.12 s

Coyote time is named for the cartoon coyote who stands on air until he looks down. Players believe they jumped from the ledge; the game agrees. Buffering means a jump pressed slightly before landing fires the instant she touches down, rather than being silently discarded.

Both make the game objectively easier. Both are almost universally described by players as the game feeling more responsive rather than easier — because they remove failures the player did not believe they had earned.

These are design, not bug fixes

Budget for them, and tune them by feel. Both numbers above were arrived at by playing, not by calculation.

13

Failure has to be legible

When a player dies they must know why within a fraction of a second, or the death feels arbitrary and they blame the game. This project uses one rule, without exception:

The rule

Hearts are for things that hit you. Geometry is for things you hit.

What happenedConsequenceWhy
Touched a fly or spiderLose a heartRecoverable — five chances
Fell down a pitDeadGeometry. You hit it
Ran into a wallDeadGeometry. You hit it
Touched the green sludgeDead6 damage against 5 hearts — lethal whatever your health

That last row is inherited from the original and kept deliberately, because it is what makes the sludge read as poison rather than as another enemy. A hazard that sometimes kills you is a hazard players will gamble against.

The rule also settles arguments in advance. When permanent upgrades were added, one grants a ghost that absorbs a hit — and the immediate question was whether it should absorb poison. No: poison is the game's one unambiguous threat, and softening it would blunt the only thing that is never negotiable.

14

Difficulty is a curve, not a list

The original's difficulty was a hand-written list of level segments, growing from five to nine. Authored, finite, identical every play. An endless game needs difficulty as a function of distance.

Two details stop this feeling mechanical:

  • A couple of easy pieces stay eligible forever. Without this, difficulty becomes a wall and every late run feels identical. With it, the game breathes.
  • The same piece never appears twice in a row. The cheapest possible fix for the "wait, I have seen this" feeling.

Selection is weighted toward the current target rather than picked flatly from everything allowed — otherwise the easiest piece, always eligible, would dominate forever.

Endless content from eight levels

Only a window of level exists at once: a few thousand units ahead, rather less behind. As the player advances, a new piece is appended in front and pieces behind are recycled — the same objects reused rather than rebuilt, which is what keeps the frame rate steady.

Where the leverage is

Eight well-made pieces plus a good selector produce more play than twenty pieces in a fixed order. Spend your effort making each piece composable — able to follow anything and be followed by anything — rather than making more of them.

15

Proving the levels are possible

If levels are assembled by machine, the machine can generate something impossible. A gap wider than the jump is not a difficulty spike; it is a dead end.

So this project has an autopilot — a bot that plays the game, jumping where the course requires it. A course it cannot clear is a failing test rather than a furious player. It earned its keep immediately by finding three genuine design flaws:

  1. Gaps were placed without looking up. The analysis checked the floor was clear but not what was above it — so a gap could appear under a low shelf, with nowhere to jump.
  2. The wall test ignored the player's height. It asked whether Pepper could get over an obstacle without accounting for her being 208 units tall.
  3. Air jumps aimed at unreachable shelves. The bot spent its second jump reaching for a platform outside the envelope, and fell.

Every one would have shipped. None would have been caught by a conventional test, because each was about the interaction of individually correct pieces.

Practical

If any part of your content is generated, build something that plays it. It does not need to play well — it needs to play at all, and to complain when it cannot.

What art you actually need

The complete inventory of this game. It is less than most people expect.

Particle effects3Sparks, clouds, circles
Audio0The original shipped none at all

The character set is bigger than you think

This is what designers most often under-scope. One player character needs every state the game can be in — seven states, thirty frames, for one character. Then the companion needs the same set minus attack.

One character, fully animated
Idle — title screen, waiting4
Run — the default10
Jump — rising3
Double jump — must look different or it reads as a glitch2
Fall — descending3
Hit — taking damage1
Attack — casting7
Your state diagram is your animation list. Draw it, count the boxes.

Enemies each need idle and death — 3 and 6 frames here. The death animation being twice as long is not an accident: killing something is the reward, so it gets the frames.

Then pickups with a shared sparkle drawn behind them; tilesets separated by purpose (remember enemies are painted as tiles, so they need tileset entries as well as sprites); UI panels and buttons as 9-patch images with stretchable middles, so one button image serves every size; and hearts in both full and empty states.

Two traps

Fonts. Bitmap fonts are images of characters, and these contain only ASCII. The moment you want accents or a non-Latin script they must be regenerated. Decide about localisation before commissioning fonts.

Audio. The most commonly abandoned category, and it costs more than its size suggests — sound is most of what makes a jump feel like a jump. The minimum is seven cues and a mute toggle that is remembered.

Two that designers routinely leave out of plans and then need anyway. The shell — title, pause, settings, game over — is not glamorous and is a substantial chunk of work; this project spent an entire milestone on it and still shipped broken buttons three times. And error handling: when something breaks for a stranger they see a black rectangle and leave. An apology and a reload button costs an hour.

The order to build in

Nine milestones, each one playable on its own.

The two rules that produced this

Every milestone ends playable. Not "compiles" — playable, something a person can pick up and form an opinion about. That is what makes feedback possible at every stage instead of only at the end.

Whatever is most likely to be wrong comes first. The riskiest thing in a runner is whether the jump feels good, and it is the hardest thing to retrofit. So it came first — and it came as grey rectangles.

The most transferable idea here

Milestone one had no art at all. Coloured boxes jumping onto coloured boxes, tested by playing it for thirty seconds and asking "does this feel right?" If it had not, changing it would have cost nothing, because nothing was built on top yet. Prove the feel before you buy the art.

The rest follows: art after feel, because art is expensive and would be redone if the feel changed. Content after art, because you cannot judge a level's readability with rectangles. Danger after content, because hazards need somewhere to be. Powers after danger, because a spell needs something to be used against. The shell after gameplay, because a menu wrapping a game that is not fun is wasted work. Polish last, because it is the only thing safe to cut.

Decide your numbers before you build

Every tunable value in this game lives in one file, and each records why it has that value. You do not need to read code to steal this: keep a one-page tuning sheet — every number that affects how the game feels, in one place, each with a reason.

Then arguments become concrete. "Jumps feel floaty" becomes "gravity is 3600, should it be 4000?" And six months later you know why a number is 0.1 and not 0.15.

At minimum your sheet needs: gravity, jump strength, run speed, forgiveness windows, hitbox size, maximum health, damage values, tile size, and your jump envelope.

Where to read next

Design

  • Steve Swink, Game Feel — the book on why controls feel good. Directly relevant: this whole project turns on jump arcs and forgiveness windows.
  • Jesse Schell, The Art of Game Design — the standard broad text.
  • Game Maker's Toolkit — start with the platformer-feel videos; coyote time and buffering are covered visually.
  • GDC talks on Celeste and Spelunky — Celeste for jump tuning and forgiveness, Spelunky for procedural levels that stay fair. Exactly the problems here.

Technical, for a designer who wants to follow the conversation

  • Fix Your Timestep! — the canonical explanation of the idea in section 02. The first part is readable by anyone.
  • Phaser — the framework here; the examples are the real documentation.
  • MDN Game Development
  • Red Blob Games — the clearest technical writing in games.
  • Tiled — free, standard, and a designer's tool rather than a programmer's. Learn it.

Art, assets and licensing

  • David Revoy's tutorials — the Pepper&Carrot artist, on Krita.
  • Kenney — enormous, genuinely free, good for placeholders that are not rectangles.
  • OpenGameArt — varied; check each licence individually.
  • Creative Commons — worth twenty minutes before building on someone's art.

The shortest version

Eight things

  • Draw your state machine. It is your animation list.
  • Write your tuning sheet before you build.
  • Build the feel first, in grey rectangles.
  • Measure the jump. Design every level against that measurement.
  • Make each milestone playable, and play it.
  • Keep the rules separate from the picture.
  • Do not cut audio and the shell — everyone does, and it shows.
  • Sort out licences before you build on someone's work.
